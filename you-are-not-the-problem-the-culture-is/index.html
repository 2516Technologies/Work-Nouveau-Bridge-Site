You are not the problem. The culture is.

May is Mental Health Awareness Month. And every year, the conversation follows a familiar pattern: tips for managing stress, reminders to practise self-care, encouragement to "reach out if you're struggling."

All of it well-intentioned. Most of it placing the responsibility on the individual.

This year, we want to say something different.

If you are a professional who has spent months, maybe years, pushing through anxiety, swallowing overwhelm, performing fine in meetings while barely holding it together behind the scenes: the problem is not you. It was never you.

The problem is the culture that made that the only option.

What Professional Culture Asks of Us

Most workplaces operate on an unspoken contract. The terms are never written down, but everyone understands them: show up, produce, be available, don't let anything personal interfere with your professional performance.

Within this contract, mental health is treated as a private matter - something you manage on your own time, ideally without anyone at work knowing about it. 

  • Anxiety becomes "I just need to be more organised."
  •  Burnout becomes "it's a busy period." 
  • Depression becomes "I'm a bit tired lately."

We have become expert translators of our own suffering into language that is professionally acceptable.

And the cost of that translation - the energy it takes to perform fine when you are anything but -  is enormous. It is a tax levied on the people who are already carrying the most.

The Stigma Hiding in Plain Sight 

Mental health stigma in professional spaces rarely looks like discrimination. It looks like culture.

The manager who responds to emotional honesty with discomfort. The performance review that mentions "resilience" as a development area for someone who was simply honest about struggling. The understanding that vulnerability belongs outside office hours.

It looks like the reward structures that celebrate the person who works through illness, who never misses a deadline no matter the personal cost, or who is always available. These behaviours get called "committed" and "professional." What they actually are is unsustainable, and the people modelling them are usually the ones closest to breaking.

The message is this: your mental health is your business. Keep it that way.

Why “Just Reach Out” Isn’t Enough 

We tell people to ask for help. Businesses might put helpline numbers in the intranet footer, or host a webinar in May.

But asking for help requires believing that it is safe to do so. 

And in many workplaces today, it’s not, because the culture signals that struggling is a weakness and weakness is a risk.

People do not disclose mental health difficulties at work because they fear being seen as unreliable, passed over for opportunities, managed out. Often, those fears are not irrational. They are based on watching what happened to someone else who was honest.

Telling people to "just reach out" without changing the environment into which they are reaching is not support. It is the appearance of support.

What Actually Changes Things 

Culture change in this space is not complicated. It is not easy, but it is not complicated.

It starts with leaders being honest about their own experience of work. Not performatively, not with a vulnerability that is carefully managed and ultimately still professional, but genuinely. "I found that period difficult. I didn't handle it as well as I could have. Here's what I wish I'd done differently."

It continues with organisations examining what they actually reward - not what the values document says, but what gets people promoted, praised, and chosen.

If the people who rise are consistently the ones who sacrifice everything else, that is the message your culture is sending.
