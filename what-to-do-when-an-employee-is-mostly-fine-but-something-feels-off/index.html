What to Do When an Employee Is “Mostly Fine” … but Something Feels Off

Not every difficult employee situation is a clear black and white thing.

Some of the most common (and draining) ones involve people who are, by most measures, doing fine. They show up. They complete their work. There's no obvious performance issue to point to. And yet something about the dynamic leaves you feeling a bit uncertain, though you can't articulate it. 

You might notice it in small, sometimes less than obvious ways: you replay conversations afterward, you take back tasks and projects, you have an instinct to double check things even when it’s not necessary. Nothing is broken. But something isn't right.

And because it’s not obvious, you do what most thoughtful, well-intentioned leaders do.

You say nothing and you wait.

You tell yourself it’s probably just a phase. That things will smooth out. That you don’t want to overreact or make someone feel bad when they’re mostly doing fine.

So the feeling lingers.

The Cost of Avoiding Feedback

Most business owners and leaders avoid giving direct feedback because they care. Who wants to be the bad guy, right?  

There are lots of reasons that someone avoids giving feedback.Maybe they are trying to maintain trust and morale. Or they hope it will resolve itself. And sometimes, there is fear that saying something might create a bigger problem than the one they started with. 

We also aren’t necessarily taught how to have difficult or uncomfortable conversations, so many of us shy away from them. Instead of addressing the discomfort, it gets carried.

You’ve seen this in action. The manager who works around the employee instead of with them. They lower expectations without saying so. And over time, the low-level frustration grows. 

Meanwhile, the employee never gets a chance to actually learn, grow or develop their skills. And chances are that they've already picked up on the awkwardness, but without context it’s useless. Eventually, their confidence will start to take the hit. 

What could have been a simple conversation becomes heavier simply because it was postponed.

Clear performance problems are straightforward. Make 10 widgets this month. Call 5 prospects this week. 

Strong performers are easy. But the “mostly fine” employee lives in a gray space where there’s no obvious rulebook and no clean trigger for action.

Owners worry: Am I being too picky? Am I missing something? What if I say something and I’m wrong?

That fear keeps people stuck between doing nothing and doing too much.

We’re here to tell you that clarity is rarely the wrong move.

What Actually Helps

You don’t need a formal performance process or a perfectly worded script. You don’t need to label the situation or escalate it. In fact, at this stage, those things can do more harm than good. 

What you need is a human conversation that puts it on the table. Name the discomfort.

Tell the employee what you’re noticing - not what you’re assuming. Focus on patterns and objective observations, not judgments, conclusions, or whatever story you’ve created in your mind. When you talk about what you’re seeing, rather than what you think it means, the conversation stays grounded and fair, and reduces the risk of putting the other person on the defensive.

And then let the employee take it from there. Maybe they share context you didn’t have. Maybe they talk about obstacles or confusion. Sometimes they’re relieved you brought it up, because they felt the tension too but didn’t know what was going on. Or maybe they’re completely surprised and have no idea what you’re talking about!

While their response matters, what you’ve actually just unlocked is opening up the conversation. 

And that gives you the chance to reset the whole situation. Bring in more clarity, define what “good” looks like, and what changes you need for things to feel solid again.

The Part Most People Get Wrong

There’s never going to be a perfect moment to initiate this conversation. 

You’ve probably heard the phrase that “direct is kind.” The truth is that waiting doesn’t make feedback kinder or easier. It makes it heavier. What started as a small, fixable misalignment can turn into weeks or months of doubt.

Addressing something early isn’t harsh. It’s respectful. Direct is kind.

A Different Way to Think About It

Giving feedback doesn’t make you the bad guy.

Letting someone continue without clarity, while your confidence in them fades, is far more damaging for both of you.

Most employees don’t expect perfection. They want direction and to know where they stand. And all of us want the opportunity to improve before assumptions harden into decisions.

If this situation feels familiar, you’re not alone. And you’re not failing as a leader. You’re simply in a tricky part of running a business.
