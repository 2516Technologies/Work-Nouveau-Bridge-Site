Election Day: What Employers Should Know Before November 3

Election Day in the US is Tuesday, November 3. With midterm races, ballot measures, and local elections all on the ballot, many employees may see this election as directly connected to their communities and daily lives. With this in mind, it’s fair to assume that some of your employees will ask for time off to vote. Here’s how to be ready.

What the Law Requires
There’s no federal requirement to give employees time off to vote, but that’s only part of the picture. Most states have their own voting leave rules, and they vary a lot. Some require a certain number of hours off, unpaid, while some have paid time off requirements. Some only apply when an employee’s schedule doesn’t leave enough hours to vote while polls are open. A few have no requirement at all. 

Notice rules differ too, and some states let employers decide when during the shift the time is taken. Even where no leave is required, some states still protect employees from being penalized for voting.

If your team works across state lines, check the rules wherever your people work, along with any local ordinances. Nonprofit VOTE’s State Requirements for Time Off to Vote is a helpful starting point. Confirm the details with your state labor department or employment counsel before you set a policy.

Who Might Need Flexibility
Most employees can vote before or after work. But not everyone’s schedule fits neatly around polling hours, which vary by state and sometimes by locality. Employees who may not have a realistic window include:

  • Long or overnight shifts - Someone on a 12-hour shift, or a shift that starts before polls open and ends after they close, may have no time to vote in person.
  • Long commutes - Employees who live far from work may need to vote near home, and the drive can eat up the time around their shift.
  • Caregivers - School drop-off, child care pickup, or caring for a family member can fill the time before and after work.
  • Multiple jobs -  Employees who go straight from one job to another may have no gap at all.
  • Accessibility needs -  Some voters need more time to get to the polls or to vote, especially if transportation is limited.

The best approach is to look at your schedules now. Identify who may be squeezed, and give managers a clear way to say yes before requests start coming in.

Pick an Approach and Share It
Common options include:

  • Encouraging voting outside work hours and offering excused, unpaid time when it’s requested in advance
  • Providing a set amount of paid time to vote across the board
  • Providing flexibility during the day on election day, letting employees start late or leave early

Whichever you choose, make sure it meets the requirements in every state where you have employees, then tell managers now so every request gets the same answer. Remind supervisors to keep the conversation neutral and never ask how or whether someone plans to vote.

Why Flexibility Pays Off
Even when it’s not required, flexibility builds trust. Employees remember how leaders respond to requests that matter to them. A quick, consistent “yes, here’s how” lets your team know that you respect their lives outside of work. 

For mission-driven organizations especially, supporting civic participation can be an easy way to show your values in action.
