Designing Benefits That People Actually Value

In today's competitive talent landscape, organizations still struggle with attraction, retention, and engagement. The numbers tell a revealing story. Bureau of Labor Statistics data shows that on average, companies in the US spend about $27,000 per employee per year to provide benefits.  But research shows that just over 50% of employees are satisfied with their benefits. 

This disconnect represents both a challenge and a huge opportunity. By reimagining how benefits are designed, delivered, and communicated, organizations can use their benefits package as benefits from a cost center into a value driver.

The traditional approach to benefits (offering identical packages to all employees regardless of their individual circumstances) is increasingly misaligned with today's workforce realities:

  • Today's workforce spans five generations with vastly different life stages, priorities, and needs
  • Non-traditional families, caregiving responsibilities, and varied financial situations create unique needs
  • Remote, hybrid, and flexible working patterns demand different types of support
  • What constitutes "valuable" differs dramatically across employee segments

And when benefits don’t align with employee needs or expectations, companies will see lower retention, engagement,and productivity.

Smart employers will recognize that today’s workforce has diverse family structures, cultural backgrounds, and life circumstances, rather than assuming traditional norms. So they’ll design benefits to meet those diverse needs, including  expanded definitions of family beyond the traditional nuclear families, ensuring cultural sensitivity in holiday and leave policies, and a recognition that the team will have diverse financial circumstances and challenges

Strategies for Creating Benefits That Deliver Value

1. Conduct Segmentation Analysis

Rather than treating all employees as having identical needs, consider analyzing your  workforce to identify meaningful segments with distinct benefit priorities.

Effective segmentation considers factors beyond basic demographics, including life stage and family situations, financial circumstances, career aspirations, development needs, wellness priorities, and work arrangement preferences.  

Based on employee demographic and survey data, you can create a few core employee personas representing different segments, and develop targeted benefit bundles to address each segment’s priorities. 

Real-world example: Financial services company Synchrony Financial created five core employee personas and designed specific benefits packages addressing each group's priorities—from young professionals focused on student loan assistance to mid-career parents needing family support to pre-retirees concerned with healthcare transitions.

2. Create Flexible Frameworks

Instead of benefits structures that are one-size-fits-all, consider general benefits frameworks, with flexibility for employees to direct resources toward their own highest priorities. For example, you could establish some non-negotiable core benefits to ensure fundamental protection and wellbeing, but add on an allocation mechanism for discretionary benefits. Build in annual or semi-annual re-enrollment windows to accommodate employees’ changing life circumstances. 

An example of this approach comes from Salesforce, which implemented a "Flex Benefits" program where all employees receive core insurance coverage plus a flexible spending allocation they can direct toward options ranging from additional vacation days to professional development to wellness services.

4. Support Holistic Wellbeing
 The best benefits look beyond physical health to support mental, emotional, and social wellbeing. Modern employees want access to resources like therapy and counseling, mental health days, caregiving support, and community or affinity groups. Employers should also consider wellness stipends or subsidies that can be used flexibly.

Real-world example: Microsoft offers generous mental health coverage, access to virtual therapy sessions, and a wellbeing allowance employees can use for a wide range of health-supportive activities.

5. Communicate Benefits Effectively
 Even the best-designed benefits are wasted if employees don’t understand how they apply to their lives, or worse don’t even know about them in the first place. Clear, accessible communication is essential. This means moving away from long, jargon-heavy plan documents and toward interactive, personalized resources: benefit dashboards, short explainer videos, and targeted campaigns timed around life events.

Real-world example: Adobe revamped its benefits communication strategy by creating a mobile-friendly portal with personalized recommendations and “moments that matter” prompts, leading to a significant increase in employee awareness and utilization.

The Bottom Line

Designing benefits that people actually value is not about offering more, it’s about offering better. By segmenting employee needs, building in flexibility, prioritizing financial and holistic wellbeing, and communicating clearly, organizations can transform their benefits from an overlooked cost to a true driver of attraction, retention, and engagement.

Companies that invest the time and creativity to align benefits with the real lives of their people will not only win the talent game but also build stronger, more resilient workplaces for the future.
