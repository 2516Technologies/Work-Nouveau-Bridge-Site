HR Isn’t a Department

Author: Angela Smith

What comes to mind when you hear “Human Resources?” 

Probably some outdated image of endless policies, paperwork, and compliance checklists. Or those are the people you go to when you need to order a cake for a colleague’s birthday. Or maybe you picture a department that only shows up when something goes wrong, focused more on rules than on people.

Those images feel really distant, irrelevant, outdated … or just plain onerous. Which is why a lot of small businesses and nonprofits opt to not have HR. “That’s for bigger companies,” they may say. 

But surprise – If you employ people, you already have HR. Maybe not as a function or a title. But it shows up  in the everyday decisions that shape how people experience working with you.

HR Shows Up Long Before It Becomes Formal

In small companies, HR rarely starts as a department. It shows up differently. For example:  

  • How you handle time off requests
  • How clearly roles and expectations are defined
  • What happens when someone struggles
  • How decisions are explained (or not)
  • Whether policies are consistent or depend on who’s asking

These may feel small or informal, but they shape the day-to-day experience of work. They signal what’s valued, who is trusted, and whether fairness exists in practice. Over time, they influence morale, performance, retention, and trust in leadership.

None of this requires a formal HR team. But all of it determines whether people feel respected, and treated fairly. 

Informal Doesn’t Have to Mean Inconsistent

Many small organizations pride themselves on being flexible and informal. And that’s great! It allows you to respond quickly and adapt to change faster. It attracts a type of employee who likes to move fast and get things done, and keeps the doors open to possibility (instead of being bogged down by bureaucracy). 

That flexibility can be a real strength,  but only if it’s paired with clarity.

Flexibility without clarity can quickly turn into confusion and frustration. Decisions can feel inconsistent. Policies and practices are decided on a whim. In the end, people are left guessing: What’s allowed? What’s not? Why was this approved for someone else but not me? 

People don’t need dozens of policies. They need to know what to expect. 

True flexibility works best when it’s grounded in clear guidelines and transparent communication. 

YOU are the HR Signal

HR rarely lives in a department or a handbook. It lives in leadership behavior. Employees notice how questions are answered, how concerns are handled, and where boundaries are drawn. Every response sends a message.

When you follow through on what you say, explain decisions (even the unpopular ones), admit when you don’t have an answer yet, and apply expectations evenly, you’re practicing good HR. 

And when hard conversations are avoided, rules shift informally, or everything is left at “we’ll figure it out later,” people notice that too. 

Those patterns shape trust.

One of the biggest misconceptions about HR is that it has to be complex to be effective. In reality, small organizations benefit most from a short set of clearly written, plain-language guidelines. People don’t need corporate jargon or a 50-page handbook. They need to understand how time off works, what flexibility really means, what good performance looks like, and how concerns can be raised and addressed. 

And it’s ok if you don’t have a formal HR team. You may not need one yet, but you can start where you are. Write down the rules you’re already following. Clarify expectations for managers. Commit to consistency, even when it’s inconvenient. Choose transparency over perfection. 

HR isn’t something you “add” later. It’s something you practice every day.

It’s about how you treat people.
